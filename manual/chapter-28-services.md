# Chapter 28: Services: time, network, updates

Part 6 began with the kernel: Chapter 22 designed the small core at the center of the system, Chapter 23 designed the boot, Chapter 24 designed processes and scheduling, Chapter 25 designed memory in the kernel, Chapter 26 designed the filesystem, and Chapter 27 designed the drivers. Chapter 26 also designed the first service without calling the chapter that: the filesystem service, a program that owns the disk's records and answers every program that needs a file. This chapter designs the other three services the book has named, time, network, and updates, and states the shape all four share.

The roadmap gives this chapter no sections, so it is built the way Chapter 27 built its drivers: first the shape every service shares, then one part per service, in the roadmap's order. Time keeps the clock set. Network gives programs a way to talk to other machines. Updates fetch, check, and install newer versions when the user asks.

One warning, stated up front the way Chapters 4 through 27 stated their own. This chapter designs the services. It does not build any of them. No service exists yet, and no claim in this chapter says one does. The chapter also claims no measured improvement on real hardware, so no scoreboard table is due. The numbers here are arithmetic on the chapter's own examples, counted by hand: checks, calls, bytes. No time is claimed anywhere in this chapter, because no service has been measured on the target machine, and Chapter 3 forbids numbers without a method. The measured proof comes in Chapter 31, on the real PC.

### The shape every service shares

Start with what a service is, in one sentence. A service is a program the system keeps for one shared job, started by the kernel when the job is first needed, that owns the records of its job, and that other programs reach through the kernel.

Chapter 26 designed the first one in full, and it is the standing example for everything in this part. The filesystem service owns the folder records and the free record's truth, it is the one writer of the records it owns, it starts when first needed and not at boot, and programs reach it through the core library's file and folder routines. The three services in this chapter follow the same shape, and the shape is stated here once, for all four.

A service is a program. It takes the next number in line when it starts, the way section 24.1 hands numbers out, and it holds its own runs in the memory record, the way section 25.1 hands runs out and Chapter 25's closing fixed: services are programs with their own runs and their own numbers. It is written in the same language, in the same middle form, translated by the same translator, and started by the kernel through the loader's eight steps from section 15.1, the way every program starts. It waits its turn for a core like any program, the way section 24.2 shares the two cores fairly. There is nothing special about a service's code. What is special is its job.

A service starts when first needed, not at boot. Section 23.2 fixed that for the filesystem service and the time service: the kernel starts the service from its fixed place the first time a program asks for its job, and asks it. Starting a service at boot would mean reading its file for a job nothing at the prompt has asked for. Each service's own file sits at a fixed place, the way the filesystem service's does, because the kernel must find it before the service exists to ask; Chapter 30 fixes those places and counts them when the image is built.

There is one service per job, one instance. Two filesystem services would keep two folder records, and the records would disagree. Two time services would keep two times. So the kernel starts a service once, and while it runs, every ask for its job goes to the one instance. A second program asking a busy service waits, served in the order it asked, the guard shape section 27.1 fixed for devices applied one level up, because a busy service is not a failure either.

A service does its job only when asked. It does not run in the background, it does not check anything on its own schedule, and it does nothing between asks, because section 2.4 forbids hidden work. This rule is the sharpest for updates, where section 14.2 already fixed that the tool never checks for updates on its own schedule, and it holds for all four alike: the filesystem service answers reads and writes when asked, the time service sets the clock when asked, the network service carries bytes when asked, and none of them starts work the user did not ask for.

A service's failures are error values, in plain words, the way section 7.3 designed errors, with the codes from section 16.3 where they fit. A service that cannot do its job hands back an error naming what happened, and the program that asked decides what to do, the way every program deals with an error. A service does not crash its asker, and it does not hide a failure behind a made-up answer.

A service logs its acts, the way section 17.1 designed the log layer: one line per act, in plain words, in its own log beside its file, held in the 1,024 byte buffer under the 64 KB limit. And it follows section 16.3's rule, log the act, never the secret, which means something specific for each service. The filesystem service logs that a file was read, not the file. The network service logs that a talk opened, how many bytes it carried, and that it closed, never the bytes themselves. The update service logs which package it fetched and whether the checks passed, never anything inside the package beyond its name and version. The time service logs each set and where the time came from. The user can read every service's log, measure what it costs, and turn logging off, because section 2.4's test asks whether the user can turn it off.

Say what a service is not, because the three differences are the design. A service is not part of the kernel. The kernel starts it, guards it, and hands it memory, the way it does for any program, and a service can end: the finish routine from section 15.2 runs, its runs are given back, and the next ask for its job starts it again. What happens to the job while the service is down follows the filesystem service's shape: the records on the disk are the truth, and they survive the service. The folder records are on the disk. The clock's count is in the clock device. The name record the network service keeps is a file. What does not survive is work in flight: a talk the network service was holding ends when the service ends, and the programs on it get an error on their next ask, stated plainly here so no program trusts a talk to outlive the service.

A service is not a driver. A driver talks to a device and knows nothing above the device. A service runs over a driver, or over the filesystem, and knows the job: the filesystem service runs over the disk driver, the network service runs over the network driver, and the time service runs over the clock, the layering Chapter 27 fixed. And a service is not a library. The core library is code the call reaches inside the system, shared by every program. The shell's tools from section 21.1 are routines joined into the shell's own file. A service is neither: it is a separate running program, with its own number and its own memory, that other programs ask through the kernel, and the asking is the whole of the connection.

### The time service

Start with the job, in one sentence. The time service keeps the count the clock driver reads set to the world's time, the promise Chapter 27 made when it fixed that the clock driver only reads the device.

Recall the chain a program's `now` travels, because the service is not in it. Section 16.2 fixed that `now` asks the system, section 22.2 worked the call through: the program calls the kernel, the kernel reads the clock through the clock driver, and the count comes back, 3 steps, 2 crossings, 0 copies. The time service is not in that chain, and a program asking for the time does not start it. The service is needed only when the time is set or checked against the world, which is rarely, and that is the honest shape: of the four services, this is the one needed least often, and it costs nothing while it is not needed, because it is not running.

Say what setting the time means, because one shape is fixed here. The clock device holds a count of seconds. The world's time is a count too, the same count section 16.2 named: seconds since the start of 1970. Setting the time means making the device's count the world's count. The service learns the world's count, from the user or from the network, in the order below. Then it asks the kernel to set the clock, and the kernel writes the new count to the clock device itself: the one write the clock ever takes, done by the kernel's own hand at the service's ask, because only the kernel talks to hardware, the way section 22.1 fixed. The clock driver's one call stays what Chapter 27 fixed, reading the count; the set is the kernel's act on the device, not a second driver call. After the set, the clock driver's count call returns the world's count, because the device holds it, and every `now` after it is the world's time with no service in the chain. The service holds no standing copy of the time: the device holds the count, one copy, in the one place that tends it. What the service owns is the account of its sets: each set is an act in its log, naming the count it set and where the time came from, user or network, so the user can see every change the clock has ever been given.

Where the world's time comes from, in order. First, the user. The user tells the service the time, through the shell: the user states the time in plain words, the service turns it into the count, and the set runs. The user's word is the first source because the machine belongs to the user, the way section 3.2 said, and a clock the user set is a clock the user trusts. Second, the network, when there is one. When the user asks the service to check the time, the service asks another machine whose job is keeping time, through the network service, and takes the count that machine hands back. This chapter names that as asking, and designs nothing about the asking itself: the wire details belong to the machines that keep time, not to this book. Third, before either has spoken, the count the clock device kept while the machine was off. The clock is hardware and it keeps counting while the machine is off, so at boot the count is whatever the device held, and section 23.2 sets the clock up from it. That count is the best the machine has until the user or the network corrects it, stated plainly as the honest limit: a machine that has never been told the time runs on its device's word alone.

Work one set through, with the count from section 16.2. At the start of 15 November 2026, the world's count was 1,794,700,800. Say the device reads 1,794,700,000, because it has drifted. The difference is 1,794,700,800 minus 1,794,700,000, which is 800 seconds. The user tells the service the world's time. The service computes the difference, 800, asks the kernel to set the clock to 1,794,700,800, and the kernel writes the count to the device. Four acts in all: the user's ask, the service's kernel call, the kernel's write, and the log line naming the set and its source. From then on, `now` hands back the world's count, and the service's work is done until it is asked again.

Say what a set does to programs already running, in the open, because a set is a change the user makes and its effects are the user's to see. A program holding a count from before the set holds the old count: nothing reaches into the program and changes its values, because that would be hidden work. `elapsed` from section 16.2 measures the difference between two counts, and a set between the two counts is inside the difference. If the set moved the count forward by 800, a program that reads `elapsed` across the set sees 800 seconds that were not work. If the set moved the count back past the program's starting count, `elapsed` hands back its error, code 6, the time is after now, the way section 16.2 fixed it. That is the honest arithmetic of a clock that can be set: `elapsed` measures counts, and the user who sets the clock knows the counts moved, because the set is the user's act and it is in the service's log.

Say what the time service does not do. It does not set the time on its own schedule: no quiet correction in the background, because section 2.4 forbids it, and the network check happens only when the user asks the service to check. It does not change the count by small steps to hide a correction: a set is one act, visible in the log, at the size it is. And it does not invent a time when neither the user nor the network has given one: before the first set, the device's count stands as it is, and the service says so.

### The network service

Start with the job, in one sentence. The network service gives programs a way to talk to other machines, over the network driver from Chapter 27, which knows bytes and nothing else.

Chapter 27 fixed the division. The network driver answers two calls, send bytes and bytes arrived, and it knows no names, no addresses, and no other machines. Everything above bytes is this service's job, and the service adds three things. Names and addresses. Talks, held apart. And order: the bytes of one talk handed back in the order they arrived.

Names and addresses first. A program names the machine it wants to talk to, in plain words, the name the user uses. The wire needs an address, the mark that picks out one machine from the others, and turning a name into an address is the service's knowledge. The shape is fixed here, stated as this chapter's design, and it is kept small on purpose. The service keeps one record, a file in the filesystem, owned by the service the way section 6.1 requires, with one entry per machine the user has named: the name, and the address the user gave for it, or the address an answering machine gave when it was first reached. The service is the one writer of the record, the way the filesystem service is the one writer of the folder records. A name that is not in the record is an error, code 7, not found, from section 16.3, with a message naming the name. The service does not guess an address, and it does not look a name up anywhere else: there is no larger naming system in this design, no server of names to ask, because section 3.2's system works with no network at all, and a record the user owns covers the machines the user talks to. Adding a machine is the user's act: the user gives the service a name and its address, and the record grows by one entry.

Talks second. A program opens a talk with one machine, sends and receives on it, and closes it. The service holds the talks apart, the guard shape from section 27.1 applied one level up: two programs' bytes never mix, because each talk is its own held-apart path through the service, with its own arrived bytes kept in the order they came. A talk belongs to the program that opened it and to the service's run: if the program ends, its talks close with it, the way section 15.2 closes its files; if the service ends, every talk ends, the way the shared shape above stated.

Programs reach the service through the core library, the way programs reach the filesystem service through the file routines from section 16.1. This chapter names four new routines for the network, in the shape of that section's table, and the library grows by exactly those four.

| Routine | Takes | Hands back | If it fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| open_talk | A name | The talk | An error, code 7, if the name is not in the record; an error naming the network, if there is no network to reach |
| send | A talk and text | The number of bytes sent | An error naming the network, if there is no network to reach |
| receive | A talk | The text that has arrived on that talk, or nothing arrived | Does not fail: nothing arrived is a normal answer |
| close_talk | A talk | Nothing; the talk is released | Does not fail |

`receive` hands back what has arrived since the program last asked, in the order it arrived, or nothing arrived, which is a normal answer and not an error, the same shape the network driver gives one level down. Ownership follows section 6.1: `open_talk` makes the talk and the caller owns it, and closing it is the caller's job, or the finish routine's when the program ends. The routines are the program's side of the service: the routine asks the kernel, the kernel asks the service, the service asks the driver, and the bytes move. Four links, each doing one job.

Work one talk through, one of section 3.2's honest uses: sending a message the user wrote. The user has written a message, `hello`, 5 bytes, to a machine named in the record. The program calls `open_talk` with the name: 1 call, and the service finds the address in its record and opens the talk. The program calls `send` with the talk and the message: 1 call, the service hands the 5 bytes to the driver's send bytes, 1 driver call, and the count comes back, 5. The other machine answers, `yes`, 3 bytes, and the bytes wait with the service, held for this talk. The program calls `receive`: 1 call, and the driver's bytes arrived, 1 driver call, hands the service the 3 bytes, which the service hands to the program, in order. The program calls `close_talk`: 1 call, and the talk is released. Count the talk: 4 routine calls, 2 driver calls, 8 bytes moved, 5 out and 3 back. The service's log holds 2 lines for the whole talk, opened and closed, with the byte count, and nothing of what the bytes said, the way section 16.3 requires.

The no-network case, stated plainly, because section 3.2 fixed that the system works with no network at all. With no network there to reach, the service still starts when first asked, because starting is the kernel's answer to an ask. It finds no network, the way Chapter 27 fixed the driver's answer, and every ask gets that answer in plain words: `open_talk` and `send` hand back the error naming the network, and the program decides what to do, the way section 7.3 designs. Nothing waits for a network to appear, and the rest of the system goes on exactly as before, because nothing at the prompt needs the network.

Say what the network service does not do. It does not listen for other machines unasked: a talk that begins from the other side is accepted only when a program the user runs has asked the service to listen, and then the listening is that program's ask, held open for it, and it ends when the program ends. The service itself starts no listening. It does not retry a send on its own: a send that fails hands back its error, and sending again is the program's choice. And it does not keep what it carried: when a talk closes, the service holds nothing of the talk's bytes, only the log lines that say the talk happened and how much it carried.

### The update service

Start with the job, in one sentence. The update service, when the user asks, fetches a newer version of a program or of the system from a place the user names, checks it fully, and installs it, and only then.

Section 22.1 fixed the kernel's side: the kernel does not check for updates, because checking means reaching for the network and deciding about versions, and none of that belongs in the small core. Section 3.2 fixed the network's side: checking for an update the user asked for is one of the network's three honest uses. And section 14.2 fixed the rules this service carries out: the package format, the install steps, fetch only when the user asks and names the place, never check on its own schedule, and an update checked fully before the old version moves aside. This service is the program that runs those rules for the programs already installed, and for the system itself. It is not the build tool from section 14.1: the build tool builds a program from source on this machine and runs its tests; the update service brings a finished package from somewhere else and checks it. Building is making. Updating is fetching and trusting carefully, and the two stay apart.

The run, in order. First, the user names what to update, a program, or the system, and names the place to fetch from. No name and no place from the user, no run: the service does not choose what to update and does not choose where from. Second, the service fetches the package from that place, through the network service, the way the network service carries any program's bytes. Or, if the user already has the package as a file, on a disk or a stick the way section 14.2 said packages travel, the service reads the file, and no network is involved at all. Third, the checks run, all of them, before anything is placed, in section 14.2's order: the package magic, R I Z P, or the file is not a package and the run stops; the description, the name and version read and the name matched against what the user asked to update; the dependencies, checked against what is installed, same major and at least the named version, and a missing or too old dependency stops the run with the missing version named; and the six validity checks from section 9.4 on the program file inside the package, magic, version, sizes, bounds, references, value shapes. Fourth, only when every check passes, the new version moves into place, and the old version moves aside. Fifth, the service reports in plain words: what it fetched, what the checks found, what it placed, and what it kept.

The old version is kept, and the rule for how long is fixed here: until the user removes it. When the new version moves into place, the old version moves aside, kept on the disk under a name that says it is the old version of that program, and the record of installed packages names both, so the user can see it, run it, or remove it. The service never removes the old version on its own, because removing it is the user's act, not the service's schedule, and a service that deleted on its own would be doing hidden work. A check that fails stops the run at the check: nothing is placed, nothing moves aside, the old version stays exactly as it was and stays the installed one, and the failure is reported in plain words, the way section 7.3 designs, naming the check that failed. A failed update leaves the machine as it found it, the same clean-failure rule section 14.2 fixed for installing.

Now the hard case, the one Chapter 26 left open: updating the system's own files at their fixed places, the kernel's file behind its size record in blocks 64 to 1087, and the shell's file in blocks 1088 to 1343, from section 26.1. A program file in a folder can move aside and be replaced by the folder record changing, the way section 26.2 designed. A fixed place has no folder record: it has a size record and the file after it, and the bootloader and the kernel read the file from the place directly. The shape is fixed here, at the level this chapter owns. The new file is written and checked in the general space first: the package is fetched, the six checks run on the program file, and the file waits in the general space, complete and proven, while the working file in the place is untouched and the machine still runs on it. The swap never writes over the working file in place while it is the only copy: the old file is kept aside first, the way every old version is kept. Then the swap follows section 26.2's rule, the record written last: the new file's bytes are written into the place, and the size record is written last, in one act, with the new size, so the record never names a file that is not there whole, and the place holds either the old file whole, under its old size record, or the new file whole, under its new one. The step by step order that makes a system update unable to brick the machine, and the recovery when a swap is interrupted halfway, are Chapter 33's design, in sections 33.2 and 33.3, and this chapter points there plainly rather than half-designing them here.

Work one update through, with the package from section 14.2: the hello loop as a package, 86 bytes, 4 for the magic, 10 for the description, 72 for the program file. The user asks for the newer version of `hello`, version 1.1.0, from a place the user names, and the new package is the same size, 86 bytes. The service fetches it through the network service: 1 fetch, 86 bytes carried. Then the checks: the package magic, 1; the description read and the name matched, 1; the dependencies checked, and the package has none, so the check runs and finds nothing missing, 1; and the six validity checks on the 72 byte program file, 6. Nine checks in all: 1 plus 1 is 2, plus 1 is 3, plus 6 is 9, and every one passes. Then the placing: the new program file moves into place, 1 act, and the old version, 1.0.0, moves aside, kept under its old name, 1 act. The record of installed packages is updated, 1 act, and the report is written, 1 act. Count the run: 1 fetch, 9 checks, 4 acts of placing and reporting, and the machine ends with the new version installed and the old one kept, both named in the record.

Say what the update service does not do, because this list is the sharpest in the book and every item in it is a rule from an earlier chapter carried out. It never checks for updates on its own schedule, the way section 14.2 fixed: there is no background check, no quiet asking what is new. It never fetches from a place the user did not name. It never installs a package that failed a check, and it never places anything before the checks pass. It never removes the old version before the new one has passed, and never removes it at all on its own. And it does not run at boot: nothing in the boot reaches for the network, the way section 23.2 fixed, and the service starts when the user asks it to, like every service, and not a moment before.

The test suite grows with this chapter. Eight new tests cover the services. Two cover the shared shape: a service starts on the first ask for its job, takes the next number in line, and a second ask goes to the same instance; and a service that ends is started again by the next ask, with its on-disk records intact. Two cover time: a set by the user changes what `now` hands back, to the count that was set; and `elapsed` across a set moved backward gives code 6, the way section 16.2 fixed. Two cover network: a talk sends and receives in order, and two talks open at once never mix their bytes; and a name that is not in the record gives code 7. Two cover updates: a package that passes every check is installed, and the old version is kept aside and named in the record; and a package that fails a check installs nothing, and the old version stays installed and untouched. That is 8 new tests, and the suite grows from 161 to 169: 161 plus 8 is 169. Each test runs on the target PC, the way section 2.5 demands.

This chapter adds four new routines to the core library, the network routines named above: `open_talk`, `send`, `receive`, and `close_talk`. The library grows from 35 routines to 39. The time service and the update service name no routines: the time is read through `now` from section 16.2 and set by asking the service, and updates are asked for by running the service, so neither needs a routine of its own.

The chapter counts as done when the services run on the target PC from section 3.1: the clock set by the user matches a known time, a talk carries a message the user wrote to another machine and brings the answer back, an update installs a newer hello loop with every check passing and the old version kept, and the times are measured in Chapter 31. Until that run happens, the chapter's claims are marked plainly as waiting for the real-PC run, the way part 5 of the done test in section 3.3 requires.

Chapter 29 designs the security model: permissions in plain words, including who may ask the update service to run and who may set the time, the data the kernel reads before it lets an ask through. Chapter 30 builds the full system image and counts the services in it, their own files and their fixed places, including the filesystem service's place Chapter 26 deferred to it. Chapter 31 runs the services' tests on the real PC and measures their work. Chapter 32 benchmarks the whole stack against today's, services included. Chapter 33 designs install, update, and recovery in full: the order that makes an update unable to brick the machine, and the recovery when a swap is interrupted. Chapter 34 designs what comes after v1, and what never gets added, and a service that works unasked is on the never list.

---

The services are four programs with one shape: the filesystem service from Chapter 26 and the three designed here. Each is a program with its own number and its own runs, started by the kernel when its job is first needed, never at boot, the one instance for its job, owning its job's records, working only when asked, failing in plain words, and logging its acts and never the contents it carries. The time service keeps the clock set: the user or the network gives it the world's count, the kernel writes the count to the clock device at its ask, and `now` reads the world's time with no service in the chain. The network service adds names, talks held apart, and order over a driver that knows bytes only, through four new routines, and the library grows to 39. The update service fetches only from a place the user names, runs 9 checks before anything is placed, keeps the old version until the user removes it, and swaps the system's own files with the size record written last, the cannot-brick order left to Chapter 33. No service works unasked, and the suite grows from 161 tests to 169.
