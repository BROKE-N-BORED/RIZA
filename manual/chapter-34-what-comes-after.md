# Chapter 34: What comes after v1

The first thirty-three chapters designed one whole system: a language, a binary format, a compiler, tools, an operating system, the image that holds it all, the run that proves it, the board that scores it, and the way it is installed, updated, and repaired. This chapter designs none of that again. It designs how the system is allowed to change after its first version: what work comes first, what work never comes, and the discipline that keeps the core from growing into the thing Chapter 1 measured.

Nothing in this chapter is new machinery. Everything named here is one of three things: work an earlier chapter already owed, a gap the book already knows it has, or a refusal an earlier chapter already earned. The chapter collects those three piles, puts them in order, and fixes the gates any later version must pass. It invents no fourth pile, because a growth plan invented at the end of a book, out of wishes, is how systems start growing in the wrong direction.

One warning, stated up front the way Chapters 4 through 33 stated their own. This chapter designs the rules for what comes after v1. It performs none of it. No later version exists, no addition has been made, and the first version itself still waits for its proof: Chapter 31's run has not run and Chapter 32's board has no round. The counts used here are the counts the earlier chapters fixed, and no time, size, or score is claimed for anything that has not been measured.

There are two sections. The first collects the work list in its order, and the never list beside it. The second defines the core, fixes the gates an addition must pass to join it, and names the numbers every later version must keep printing.

## 34.1 What to add next, what to never add

Start with the job, in one sentence. The work that comes after v1 is collected here from the records the first version leaves behind, in the order it must be taken, and the work that never comes is named beside it, each refusal with the chapter that earned it.

The work list has four sources, and the order among them is the design. Fixes first, then measured losses, then the gaps the book knows, then the optimizations the book skipped. Take them one at a time.

First, Chapter 31's fails, when the run happens. Section 31.2 fixed the road back before any number existed: a fail sends the work to the chapter that owns the design, the fix is made in that chapter in the open, the image is rebuilt, and the whole run repeats from the cold boot. Those fixes are the first work of any later version, before any addition of any kind, because a promise known to be broken outranks a new ability nobody has promised yet. Chapter 31 said it in its own closing: the fails in its record, the honest losses, and what the user said at the prompt in the daily use test are this chapter's first inputs. A later version that adds a feature while a fail from the proof run stands open has its order backwards, and this chapter says so as a rule, not a preference.

Second, Chapter 32's losing rows. The board compares Riza against today's stack at its honest best, on the target machine, over eight fixed tasks and five measures, and some rows will lose. Each losing row is a named piece of work with its size on the page: the task, the measure, and the margin, all repeatable by any reader with the same machine. Work taken from a losing row is aimed at a number, not at a feeling, and when the work is done the board itself says whether the row moved, because the fix owes a before and an after like every other change. A loss nobody works on is still information: it is the map of where today's stack is genuinely better, and the map is worth having even where the work is never taken.

Third, the gaps the book knows it has, named here as undesigned, in the open, because a gap named is work waiting and a gap papered over is a surprise waiting. There are four.

A designed uninstall was never written. Chapter 14 designed installing a package: the checks, the placing, the record of what is installed. Chapters 28 and 33 designed the user removing a kept old version after an update. But removing a program and everything it brought, its file, its entry in the record of installed packages, its entry in the permission record, and its kept old versions, as one act that leaves nothing behind and breaks nothing that depends on it, was never designed end to end. Installing has a chapter. Uninstalling has this paragraph, and the difference is stated plainly so no reader mistakes the one for the other.

The record run's growth mechanics were left at a shape. Section 25.1 settled that the kernel's record of runs lives in its own run and grows the way a list grows, from section 5.2: a bigger run when it fills, the old contents copied, the old run freed. The shape is fixed and nothing past the shape is: how much bigger each growth should be, when a growth is triggered, and what a growth costs on a machine that is running programs while it happens were never designed. A long running program that asks for heap memory again and again fills the record entry by entry, and the record's growth under that pressure is open work.

The rules for sharing memory between programs were never settled. Section 6.1's ownership answers the question inside one program: every value has exactly one owner, and the owner frees it. Section 25.1 gives every run exactly one owner at the machine level too. Two programs sharing one run, one writing while the other reads, raises questions neither chapter answered: whose run it is, who may free it and when, and what the hardware's protection should say about a run with two owners. The book names this as open design work, the honest kind, stated at its strength the way Chapter 29 stated the strength of its randomness claim: inside a program the question is closed, between programs it is open, and no sentence in this book should be read as claiming otherwise.

A second kernel place is a layout question, not a feature. Chapter 33's six step swap protects the kernel's place with a zeroed size record, and its honest limit is that an interruption inside the window is answered by recovery, not by an untouched old kernel still in its place. A second kernel place, holding the other version so the boot always has a whole kernel to read, would close that window, and Chapter 30 already named growing a fixed place as one of the later layout changes this chapter weighs. Weighed here, it stays a candidate, not a decision: it is a change to the layout Chapter 30 counted, and layout changes are decided in the open with their costs counted, the way section 34.2 prices every fixed place out of the general space.

Fourth, Chapter 12's skipped optimizations. Section 12.2 named four: loop unrolling, vectorization, aggressive inlining, and speculative optimization, each skipped because its saving was a guess and its cost was certain bytes. That section also fixed how a skip ends: the skips are decisions, not laws, and if a measurement proves a skip wrong, the decision changes in the open, with the numbers. So each of the four is a candidate for a later version under one condition: the scoreboard shows the work it would save, on a named task, in a measured row. A skip admitted on that evidence still owes section 32.2's before and after, like any change. A skip admitted on a hunch is the optimizer betting, and section 12.2 already refused the bet.

The order rule gathers the four sources into one sentence: fixes before additions, measured losses before known gaps, known gaps before skipped optimizations, and nothing on the list starts until v1's own proof exists. The last clause is the load bearing one. Chapter 31's run and Chapter 32's first round are the first version's evidence that it does what the book said. Adding to a system before that evidence exists is building on a number nobody has: the addition cannot be scored, because there is no before to score it against, and it cannot be trusted, because the base it stands on has not been run. Later versions wait for the proof the way chapters waited for it, by the rule in part 5 of section 3.3.

Against that list stands the other list, and it is the shorter and more important of the two. These are the nevers: things no later version adds, each earned by a chapter that showed the cost of opening the door.

A service that works unasked. Chapter 28 fixed that a service does its job only when asked: it does not run in the background, it checks nothing on its own schedule, and it does nothing between asks. A service that starts work the user did not ask for is hidden work with a service's name on it, and it does not ship in any version.

An update that checks, fetches, or installs itself in the background. Chapters 28 and 33 fixed that updates are asked for, never automatic, and section 14.2 fixed it earlier still for packages: the tool never checks for updates on its own schedule and never phones home. The ask is the user's. A version that moves the ask into the background has taken the user's decision and made it the system's habit, and that is refused here in advance.

Permission data a program can edit. Chapter 29 fixed that the permission record is the user's writing: the user commands the changes through the shell, the kernel is the record's only writer, and a program cannot change any entry, not even its own. Data a program could rewrite to allow itself an act would not be permission at all, it would be a suggestion. No version widens that door.

An installer that takes a disk without the user's plain confirmation. Chapter 33 fixed that the install states what it takes, the whole disk, and does not begin the write until the user confirms that state in so many words. An installer that shortens that conversation to save the user a minute has spent the user's disk to buy it, and the trade is refused.

A recovery that widens a permission entry on its own. Chapter 33 fixed that recovery restores the record's first form, and any entry wider is written only when the user names it and approves it. Recovery's job is to return the system to a state the user chose, not to choose a wider one while the user is distracted by a broken machine.

A single total score for the system. Chapter 32 fixed that the board has no total row, because the five measures trade against each other and one number would hide every trade the book exists to expose. A later version that collapses the board into one score has not summarized the evidence, it has destroyed it, and section 32.2's tests already refuse a record that carries one.

A garbage collector. Chapter 6 built memory on ownership: every value has one owner, and the owner frees it by the program's own structure, with no scan, no background worker, and no pause. Section 1.3 measured what the collector costs on today's stack, a worst case pause of 2.2 seconds in the published test it cited. A collector added later would not be a feature. It would be the tax Chapter 1 counted, reinstalled by the system that was built to refuse it.

Hidden work of any kind. Section 2.4 fixed the test every part of the system must pass: can the user see it, can the user measure what it costs, can the user turn it off. Work that fails any of the three does not ship, in v1 or in any version after it. This entry is the general form of the first two, kept on the list separately because the specific forms get invented faster than they can be named.

Say what the never list is, in plain words, because it will be misread as stubbornness. It is not a mood and it is not a taste. It is a set of doors kept shut on purpose, each one shut by a chapter that did the work of showing what opening it costs, in numbers or in broken promises. The list is short because it is expensive to earn an entry: a never has to be argued by a chapter, not asserted by a preference. And say how a never could ever change, because a list that cannot change is a list that will be quietly routed around. A never changes only the way it was made: by a chapter of the same weight as the one that set it, arguing in the open, with the reason written down, the way section 3.3 requires a rule of the build to change. Section 2.4's own rule stands behind the whole list: Chapter 2 said that if a later chapter contradicts it, Chapter 2 wins, and Chapter 34 is a later chapter like any other. What no version may do is cross a never by a quiet addition, a small exception, a temporary measure that stays. That slow crossing, one reasonable exception at a time, is the exact drift this section exists to prevent, and it is prevented by making the loud way the only way.

## 34.2 Keeping the core small as it grows

Start with the job, in one sentence. The core is four things named below, everything else is a program in the general space, and this section fixes the gates an ability must pass to cross from being a program into being core.

Define the core plainly, from the book's own parts. The core is the kernel from Chapter 22, the part that starts and stops programs, owns the memory, and guards the devices, and does nothing else. It is the core library from Chapter 16, the routines every program calls because every program does that work. It is the fixed places from Chapter 30, the counted room at the front of the disk where the files the boot needs sit behind their size records. And it is the one shared form from section 2.3, the middle form the compiler, the tools, the shell, and the kernel all speak, because a second form would be a second core growing beside the first. Everything else in the system is an ordinary program: the tools, and every program the user writes or fetches, living in the general space and started, stopped, and measured like any other program. Chapter 2 named this section's job in advance: section 2.4 said the small core rule has to be defended every time, because the pressure to add to the core never stops, and it named Chapter 34 as the guard. This section is that guard, written as gates instead of as a hope.

The first rule follows from the definition: a new ability is a program first. It is written as an ordinary program in the general space, run, measured, and lived with there. It joins the core only when it must, and must is argued, not assumed. The argument has to show why a program cannot do the job: because the ability is needed before any program can run, the way the kernel is, or because every program needs it on every run, the reason the library's routines are in the library. An ability that merely works better in the core, or is tidier there, or was written by someone with the kernel open anyway, has not met the bar. Programs can do almost anything, as section 2.4 said of modules, and the burden of proof sits on the crossing, not on staying out.

An addition that claims the crossing must pass five gates, stated here as the project's standing discipline for every later version.

First, it answers a named gap or a measured loss. The name comes from section 34.1's list: a fail's fix, a losing row, a known gap, a skipped optimization the board has since justified. A wish is not a name. An addition that cannot point at its entry on the list does not start, because work with no named reason is how a core fills with answers to questions nobody on this machine asked.

Second, it is designed in a chapter, in the open, the way every part of v1 was designed. The design states what the addition does, what it must not touch, and what it costs, counted before it is built, and the chapter waits for its own proof the way the thirty-four before it waited. A core addition that arrives as code first and explanation later has reversed the only order this book trusts.

Third, it carries tests that grow the suite. The suite is the system's memory of what it promised, and an addition with no tests is a promise nobody wrote down. The tests run on the target PC, the way section 2.5 demands of everything in this book, and a failing test is a fail of the addition's chapter, the way section 31.2 assigned every fail to its owner.

Fourth, it faces Chapter 32's scoreboard, before and after, in the same round. A core addition that worsens a row is not automatically refused, because trades are real and section 1.5's rule 7 expects them. But the trade is stated in plain words beside the row, and accepted in the open, before the addition counts as finished. A core addition that quietly worsens the boot row to win the speed row has made the system's decision for it, in the dark, and the board exists to keep that from happening.

Fifth, if it wants a fixed place, or a bigger one, it pays in counted blocks out of the general space, and the count is printed before the change is made. Chapter 30's arithmetic is the standing price list: the fixed region is 1,921 blocks, and every block given to it comes out of the general space's 244,138,704. Work the price once, on the candidate from section 34.1: a second kernel place the size of the first would take 1,024 blocks, and the general space would fall from 244,138,704 blocks to 244,137,680, 244,138,704 minus 1,024. Whether that price is worth paying is a decision a later chapter makes in the open. What this chapter fixes is that the price is always stated in that form, blocks counted, before anyone argues about whether it is small.

Later versions live under the same rules as the first. The target machine is still section 3.1's machine. The budget is still section 3.2's hard $0, and the system still works with no network at all. A later version that needs a bigger machine, a paid tool, or a connection to do its work has not grown the system. It has left the system's ground, and the rules in Chapter 3 do not bend to follow it, because section 3.3 fixed that a design that breaks a rule written there is wrong, and the design changes, not the rule.

The evidence that this discipline can hold is not a promise. It is the book itself, read as a record. The core library reached 39 routines in Chapter 28, when the network service's four routines joined it, and it has not grown in the six chapters since, this one included. Chapters 29 through 33 needed a permission record, an image, a test run, a scoreboard, and three procedures, and every one of them was built as programs, records, and files in the general space, with the library's count closing unchanged at 39 each time, because the work did not need new core routines and the chapters said so instead of reaching for the core out of habit. The fixed region is 1,921 blocks of the disk's 244,140,625, which is 7,868,416 bytes of its 1,000,000,000,000, reserved whether the places are full or nearly empty, and Chapter 30 stated that cost in the open rather than hiding it. The kernel's common path was sized in Chapter 22 to fit the 64 KB instruction cache on each core, 65,536 bytes, and Chapter 31 tests the fit on the real PC, with a failure counted as a fail of Chapter 22's design, not as a reason to want a bigger cache. Small, in this book, has never been an adjective. It has always been a set of numbers with chapters holding them.

So the numbers become the standing report. Every later version prints four counts in its own record, the way Chapter 32's board prints its rows: the image's total size, the library's routine count, the fixed region's blocks, and the suite's size. The counts cost nothing to print and everything to fake, because each one can be checked against the build's report and the disk's layout by any reader. A version whose counts grew shows the growth on its face, with the chapter that argued for it named beside the change. A version that does not print the counts has hidden its growth, and hidden growth is hidden work, which section 2.4 already refused. Growth is not forbidden by this chapter. Unexamined growth is, and the examination is four numbers and a scoreboard, repeated every version, in the open.

Close the section, and the book's main text, by returning to where it started. Chapter 1 measured a stack that grew the way stacks grow: a layer added for a real reason, then never removed; a runtime loaded whole because removing the unused part was nobody's job; work repeated at every boundary because counting it was nobody's job either. Nothing in that stack was added by a fool, and it still arrived at an empty editor window holding hundreds of megabytes. The lesson was never that growth is bad. It was that uncounted growth compounds. This system's answer is not a smaller wish. It is a counted core, a never list with chapters behind it, a scoreboard that every change must face, and four numbers printed at every version so the compounding, if it ever starts, starts in public. The first version is finished when its proof runs, Chapter 31's run and Chapter 32's first round, not when its list of features feels long. What comes after that is the building and the runs, on the real PC, under these rules.

The appendices hold the final numbers, and they are the book's last word because numbers were its first. Appendix A holds the opcode dictionary, Appendix B the binary file format spec, Appendix C the middle form spec, Appendix D the glossary in plain words, Appendix E the checklists, and Appendix F the hardware notes for the Mint PC. Among them stand the final opcode, routine, and kernel call numbers, the type tag numbers, the machine code bytes, the fixed places, and the suite and library counts this chapter leaves as the first version's closing state.

The test suite grows with this chapter. Eight new tests cover the growth discipline itself, because a discipline that is not tested is a discipline that drifts, and this book tests what it trusts. Two cover the work list's order: a version record that lists an addition while a Chapter 31 fail stands open against the current image is marked out of order; and a proposed addition naming no gap, no losing row, and no justified skip is refused entry to the work list. Two cover the never list as records: a version record carrying a single total score for the system is refused as malformed, the way Chapter 32's board refuses a total row; and a change recorded as an exception to the never list, with no chapter arguing it in the open, is marked unfinished. Two cover the gates: a layout change whose record does not print the block count it takes from the general space is refused; and a library that grew while the version record's printed routine count did not move is caught as a false record. Two cover the standing counts: a version record prints the image's total, the library's count, the fixed region's blocks, and the suite's size, each matching the build's own report; and a version record missing any of the four counts is refused as incomplete. That is 8 new tests, and the suite grows from 209 to 217: 209 plus 8 is 217. Each test runs on the target PC, the way section 2.5 demands.

This chapter adds no new routines to the core library. It designs the rules under which the library may grow, and rules are not routines. The library stays at 39 routines, and the suite grows to 217 tests.

The chapter counts as done when the first later version has been made under it: v1's proof run and first scoreboard round complete, the version's work taken from those records in this chapter's order, its four counts printed in its record, and no never crossed on the way. Until that version exists, the chapter's claims are marked plainly as waiting, the way part 5 of the done test in section 3.3 requires, and this chapter waits like the thirty-three before it, by its own rule.

There is no Chapter 35. The appendices, A to F, hold the final numbers: the opcode dictionary, the binary format spec, the middle form spec, the glossary, the checklists, and the hardware notes. What comes after the book is not another chapter. It is the building, the runs on the real PC, and the versions that follow under the rules this chapter fixed.

---

The last chapter designs no machinery. It collects the growth of the system into three piles and fixes their order: Chapter 31's fails first, then Chapter 32's losing rows, then the gaps the book knows it has, a designed uninstall, the record run's growth mechanics, the unsettled rules for programs sharing memory, and a second kernel place weighed as a layout change, and last the four optimizations Chapter 12 skipped, each admitted only on scoreboard evidence. Nothing on the list starts until v1's own proof exists. Beside the list stands the never list, eight doors kept shut by the chapters that earned them: no service working unasked, no update acting on its own, no program editing permission data, no installer taking a disk without the user's plain word, no recovery widening an entry on its own, no single total score, no garbage collector, and no hidden work of any kind, and a never changes only by a chapter arguing in the open, never by a quiet addition. The core is the kernel, the core library, the fixed places, and the one shared form; everything else is a program first, and an addition joins the core only through five gates, a named reason, a chapter in the open, tests, the scoreboard before and after, and any fixed place paid for in counted blocks out of the general space. The library has held at 39 routines for six chapters and the fixed region stands at 1,921 blocks, and every later version prints those counts and two more, the image's total and the suite's size, so growth stays a fact on the page. No new routines join the library, and the suite grows from 209 tests to 217.
